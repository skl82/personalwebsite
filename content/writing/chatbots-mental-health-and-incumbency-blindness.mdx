I recently attended a series of conferences on AI and healthcare, including a discussion on AI and mental health. I was struck by the perceptible scorn, and even denial, coming from some professional and academic panelists regarding the prevalent use of generalist AI tools like ChatGPT and Claude as stand-ins for therapy. By my read, the topic was persistently framed in a negative light, with barely a mention of the possible upside; one panelist noted that the “technology wasn’t there” specifically because therapy-specific AI systems and apps hadn’t taken off yet among users. To be frank, my knee-jerk reaction went straight to the “ivory tower” trope, but upon further reflection, I was reminded of the less aggressive yet tremendously impactful lessons regarding incumbency blind spots in Clayton Christensen’s *The Innovator’s Dilemma*.

*The Innovator’s Dilemma* explains how seemingly untouchable incumbent companies can suddenly find themselves outplayed by comparatively diminutive startups. The basis of this phenomenon, per Christensen, is that established players simply lose touch with the real needs of their customer base. As incumbent firms grow, cost structures rise and legacy processes become entrenched, creating pressure to move upmarket into higher margin, more powerful technologies *within the existing, dominant paradigm*. Over time, the pace of technological progress within a given paradigm exceeds general consumer requirements (especially as prices rise). All the while, upstart companies building entirely new, more cost-effective paradigms grow beneath the incumbents. The key crossover point happens when the new technology becomes powerful enough to satisfy the needs of the original paradigm’s target consumer, at a lower price and in a more accessible fashion. An incumbent player (typically a massive bureaucracy by this point) that misses this transition usually has little recourse due to their accumulated inertia.

Consider the classic Christensen example of the shift from mainframes to minicomputers. When the latter first emerged, they were far too underpowered to touch mainframe customers, so incumbents like IBM paid little attention. However, minicomputer makers like DEC found footing in new markets that didn’t need or couldn’t afford mainframes. Those markets provided the financial resources for DEC to iterate—and bought time to ride the wave of improvement in the underlying technology—until their solutions were feature-competitive with most mainframe customer use cases. The paradigm switched, taking customers with it and leaving incumbents behind. Extrapolating the pattern, the disruptor wins not by being superior right away, but by being good-enough and more accessible, getting early traction before climbing the capability curve until it eats the ignorant incumbent’s market from below.

I believe the perspectives that I observed at this event are rooted in a similar pattern. It’s absolutely logical that domain experts have reservations about rapidly deployed, generalist tools from companies that didn’t spend a decade or more studying the field. But it’s also no secret that the existing paradigm for therapy has severe flaws—a shortage of practitioners, long wait times, and general inaccessibility to a large share of the population due to cost and location constraints. I do believe that a top-of-line, licensed therapist is still the *ideal* for everyone. But the fact of the matter is that that level of diffusion is impossible, at least for now; we simply lack the manpower.

Enter AI. ChatGPT and Claude may not have spent a decade in school, but they’ve burst onto the scene nonetheless, and the people have spoken through their usage behavior. Per a recent RAND study, 19.8% of adolescents and young adults reported receiving counseling from a mental health professional. 19.2% had used chatbots for advice or help “when feeling sad, angry, nervous or stressed.”[^rand]

Of course, usage figures only indicate the strength of demand, not the efficacy or quality of a solution; cigarettes, in fact, also have robust usage data. However, I don’t aim to tackle the quality of ChatGPT or Claude’s “therapy.” What I hope to point out is that a severe unmet need produced a major behavioral response, and meeting that response with negativity and denial—with little regard for even the possibility of upside—reads to me like the exact incumbent blind spot Christensen describes.

While the behemoth that is medical education is trying to produce enough licensed therapists who meet its extensive checklist of standards (the current paradigm), AI tools (the new paradigm) have met people where they’re at. Perhaps, in their moments of need, the average consumer doesn’t require someone who passed the NCMHCE; they just need some*thing* that will listen, or at least give the impression of listening. For $20/month, users can access a stand-in thought partner, sounding board, and companion that is available 24/7, anywhere in the world, and has effectively infinite patience (at least until the rate limit runs out).

What struck me most about the panels was a lack of acceptance of the connection between the systemic realities and the behavioral outcome. Make no mistake, there was ample acknowledgment of the system’s shortcomings, and a consensus that chatbots aren’t the ideal solution (*agreed*). Yet, I perceived *disdain* for the choice itself—for the decision to reach for a chatbot in a time of need. Ultimately, the root of the incumbents’ blind spot is their evaluation of the disruptor on *their own metrics*, not the customer’s, and I see the same ignorance brewing here.

Now, the necessary caveats. These thoughts are anchored only in my own experience, and are far from universal judgments on every mental health expert. What is good for the *average* consumer is certainly not applicable to the entire population. I have profound sympathy for those with SMIs and anyone affected by tragic chatbot-related mental health incidents. I also have deep respect for therapists, psychologists, and professional and academic subject matter experts who are giving their lives to the betterment of our mental health. Furthermore, I acknowledge that challenging questions around chatbot “engagement-baiting” and misaligned incentives at AI labs themselves may color my assessment.

Incumbent experts—like the panelists I heard at the discussion—must recognize that a *suboptimal* solution appears to have satisfied a share of average consumer need. This new reality came about because of the systemic shortcomings in the legacy therapy paradigm, and generalist chatbots aren’t going anywhere. The sooner everyone accepts this, the quicker we can craft practical mental health solutions grounded in the tools people are actually using.

[^rand]: [rand.org — Nearly 1 in 5 U.S. adolescents and young adults use AI](https://www.rand.org/news/press/2026/06/nearly-1-in-5-us-adolescents-and-young-adults-use-ai.html)
